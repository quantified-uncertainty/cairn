## Inputs

Each row is a harm mode, one way a delegated action can cause damage (see [Risk Decomposition](/delegation-risk/risk-decomposition/)). A row takes three inputs:

- **Actions per month.** How often the agent takes the action that exposes this harm. A probability means nothing until you say what it is per. Here it is per action, and the volume turns it into a monthly figure.
- **P(harm) per action, as a 90% interval.** This is your uncertainty about the rate. The calculator models it as a logit-normal with those 5th and 95th percentiles. For a point estimate, enter the same number twice. Both ends must be above zero.
- **Damage per incident, as a 90% interval.** This is the spread of cost across incidents, modeled as a lognormal. A lognormal's mean exceeds its median, and expected loss uses the mean: an interval of \$20 to \$500 has a median of \$100 and a mean of \$161. Each row's summary line shows the mean.

[Expert Elicitation](/design-patterns/tools/expert-elicitation/) covers how to get defensible intervals when you have no data.

## Two different answers

**Delegation risk** is the expected loss, $\sum_m N_m \, \mathbb{E}[p_m] \, \mathbb{E}[D_m]$ per month. This is the number to compare with a risk budget. It is also the number that adds across components and subagents (see [consolidation](/delegation-risk/runtime-accounting/#3-the-exposure-ledger)). The interval beside it reflects uncertainty about the rates only.

**Realized loss** is what a single month actually costs. For each simulated month, the calculator does three things for every row:

1. draws a rate;
2. draws a binomial number of incidents at that rate;
3. draws a damage for each incident.

It repeats this 20,000 times. The resulting distribution answers different questions: how often a month's loss exceeds the budget amount, and how bad the 99th-percentile month is.

The default example shows why the two need separating. Expected loss is about \$1,700 per month, inside the \$2,000 budget. About half of that comes from the record-disclosure row, which is expected roughly once in 12 years. Because it occurs in fewer than one month in a hundred, the 99th-percentile month is set mostly by ordinary wrong answers. With inputs like these, a run of quiet months says little about the row that dominates expected loss. Rare, high-damage harm modes therefore need an [exposure bound](/delegation-risk/runtime-accounting/) and an elicited estimate. A tally of incidents cannot tell you their rate.

## Assumptions and limits

- **Independence.** Incidents are independent given the rates, and uncertainty about different rows is independent. Correlation leaves expected loss unchanged but fattens the tail of realized loss. When harm modes share a cause, the percentiles are therefore optimistic.
- **Adversaries.** An adaptive adversary does not have a fixed rate. For the hijack and defection channels, a rate describes current attacks against current defenses. Assume the defenses fail together ([Risk Propagation](/delegation-risk/risk-propagation/#adversarial-inputs)).
- **Unlisted harms.** Harm modes you have not listed are absent from the total. The calculator prices the harm surface you enumerate, not the one you have.
- **Large incident counts.** When one row has more than 200 incidents in a simulated month, their sum is drawn from a lognormal with the same mean and variance rather than one draw at a time.
- **Squiggle export.** The export is a [Squiggle](https://www.squiggle-language.com/) model of the expected-loss part only, tested with `@quri/squiggle-lang` 0.10.0. The realized-loss simulation is not included.

## Bringing a row under budget

Expected loss is a product, so there are four levers:

- **Lower the rate** with verification and monitoring: [Trusted Monitor](/design-patterns/monitoring/#trusted-monitor), or a [Voting Tribunal](/design-patterns/structural/#voting-tribunal) whose members genuinely fail independently.
- **Lower the damage** with an [Exposure-Envelope Gateway](/design-patterns/structural/#exposure-envelope-gateway) or [Checkpoint-Rollback](/design-patterns/recovery/#checkpoint-rollback).
- **Lower the volume** by routing fewer actions through the risky path, for example sending high-stakes cases up an [Escalation Ladder](/design-patterns/structural/#escalation-ladder).
- **Split the row** into narrower components, each with smaller damage.

[Quick Start](/design-patterns/tools/quick-start/) puts these into a procedure.
