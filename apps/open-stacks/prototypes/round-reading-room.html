    About this prototype

    A deep mockup for Open Stacks: one round wall holding every work in the wiki's shared datasets, built to test what a spatial interface adds to exploring literature, on a laptop, a phone or a headset.

    What it demonstrates

    • Everything at once, in a stable place. Call number runs clockwise from the door (A to Z, then an unshelved cart of works with no LC number); each shelf tier is a band of publication years, so height is time. Every work keeps the same home on every visit, which is what spatial memory needs.
    • Labels that sharpen as you approach. From the centre you read class names on the frieze; at mid distance, subclass plates; up close, shelf-lip call ranges; at arm's length, titles on the spines. The altimeter names the level.
    • Whisper. A query lights the works that match it, dims the room, and sends a ripple across the wall that lights works sharing the matches' subject headings (subject "lamps" you can switch off).
    • Pull a book. Click a spine: it slides out, and its card gives the record, access status and the routes to read it.
    • Ask the librarian. A reading trail drawn across the wall, stop by stop. The example trail is precomputed; when opened in Claude, a live librarian builds new trails with a catalogue-search tool, and every work it names is checked against the data.
    • Unroll. The cylinder bends flat into a strata chart (call number × year), the everyday 2D view.

    Why a round room, and what the headset adds

    This is the spatial-computing piece in the gallery; its design follows the Virtual reading room chapter.

    • Walls, not clouds. Spatial memory is the strongest case for a spatial interface (virtual memory palaces: 84% recall in a headset against 75% on a monitor, Krokos et al. 2019), but freedom to place things in depth made retrieval worse (Cockburn & McKenzie 2002). So the works sit on one 2.5D wall with landmarks (the door, class pilasters, the public-domain line), never in a free-floating 3D cloud.
    • Space, not sharper text. Headsets resolve about 20–37 pixels per degree, against about 60 for normal acuity, so a headset gives more room but not more legible text. Titles therefore appear only when you are close, and the headset card is set at about 1.4° per em, above the chapter's 1° guideline.
    • Text in WebGL. Inside an immersive WebXR session the page's HTML is not drawn, so every label here is painted into textures. That is why the same room works in Quest Browser and in Vision Pro Safari (WebXR VR sessions), served from the wiki.
    • A mode, not a silo. Sessions in a headset run an hour or two, so the same wall, positions and whisper work on a laptop and a phone, and the unrolled view is the everyday tool.

    How to move

    • Desktop: drag to look along the wall; scroll to walk toward the point under the cursor; click a spine to pull it. Keys: arrows pan, +/− walk, Enter pulls the book in the centre, / whispers, Esc closes.
    • Phone: drag with one finger, pinch to walk, tap a spine.
    • Headset: point and pull the trigger (or pinch) at a book to glide to it, again to pull it; left stick glides, right stick turns and rises; grip cycles example whispers; point at the floor to return to the centre.

    The data

    • Real: 7,127 Open Library works (Search API, CC0, fetched 15 October 2026), with LC call numbers, first-publication years, reading-log counts and ebook access; 29,007 Project Gutenberg English nonfiction books (catalog feed, 15 October 2026); the Library of Congress Classification Outline. 56 catalogue works matched a Gutenberg book and are shelved once, gaining the free Gutenberg copy as a route. 36,078 works in all.
    • Estimated (by rule, not AI): Gutenberg's catalog gives only the year a book was added, so each Gutenberg book is dated from its author's life dates (birth + 0.65 × lifespan; median error about 10 years on 153 catalogue works with known dates), or a year in its title; 3,292 stay undated on the bottom plinth. Gutenberg books carry only a class (QA, BC…), so within a subclass they are shelved beside catalogue works that share a subject heading (marked ≈).
    • AI-generated: the captions of the example librarian trail (precomputed by Claude, October 2026; spot-check before relying on them) and any live trail.
    • Not modelled: holds, waiting lists and library availability. "Where to read" links go to real Gutenberg, Internet Archive, Open Library and WorldCat pages built from the record's ids.
