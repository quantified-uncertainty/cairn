Library e-resource licenses are scoped to the population a library serves; LAPL says so explicitly (below). Most of California's big systems define that population as every California resident, so a Bay Area researcher can hold several overlapping sets of licenses for the price of a few desk visits. Whether the extra visits pay depends on what you read. For technical books, one SFPL card captured all the measured coverage. For humanities and trade books, the extra cards matter.

## The short version

- **Get a San Francisco Public Library (SFPL) card first.** It is free to every California resident: apply online, then show photo ID at any branch ([SFPL](https://sfpl.org/welcome-new-cardholders), as of October 2026). Its O'Reilly login stands in for a \$499/year individual O'Reilly plan ([O'Reilly pricing](https://www.oreilly.com/pricing/), October 2026), if the library edition carries nearly all the books [~70%]. In a 33-book technical spot-check, SFPL's O'Reilly and Libby plus legal free copies covered **28/33 (85%)**.
- **For technical ebooks, the O'Reilly login is worth more than Libby.** O'Reilly held 14 of the 33 titles. SFPL's Libby held 10, mostly trade books (*Superforecasting*, *The Book of Why*).
- **Extra Bay Area cards raise humanities coverage, not technical coverage.** Across six Bay Area systems, Libby held **16/20 humanities titles** against SFPL's 10, and Santa Clara County was the best second card (+4). In the technical set the stack raised Libby's count from 10 to 18, but every added title was already readable through SFPL's O'Reilly login or a free copy, so coverage stayed at 28/33. For technical readers the extra cards buy format (Libby loans reach Kindle and Kobo e-readers; O'Reilly does not) and insurance if SFPL drops O'Reilly.
- **If you are not a California resident,** Santa Clara County Library District (SCCLD) gives a **free full-access card, including its 24/7 Online Library, to anyone with a US mailing address** who applies in person ([SCCLD](https://sccld.org/card/), October 2026). It is the only free Bay Area route for out-of-state readers I could confirm.
- **Out-of-area remote access is shrinking.** LAPL's instant e-card is now for Los Angeles City residents only, citing vendor licensing ([LAPL](https://lapl.org/library-card/borrowing-policies-and-information)), and Orlando's Orange County Library System stopped issuing fee cards on 2026-07-31 ([OCLS](https://ocls.org/using-library/types-cards/what-are-types-cards/)). Get in-person cards while they are offered.
- **JSTOR through a public library is officially walk-in only** ([JSTOR](https://about.jstor.org/products/fees/public-libraries/)), yet SFPL and Berkeley list remote proxy links. I put it at [~50%] that an SFPL card gets you JSTOR from home. Test it before buying JPASS.
- **For print-only books, use Link+ before interlibrary loan, and both before buying.** Link+ reaches 11M+ items in 64 libraries, and most requests arrive in under a week ([Oakland PL](https://oaklandlibrary.org/borrow-from-another-library/)). Only 5 of the 64 members are academic libraries, so it is weaker for graduate texts.
- **Library access does not license AI use.** O'Reilly's membership agreement forbids using its content "as input or a prompt to a machine learning or AI system" ([O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/membership-agreement/), updated 2024-10-09). Read with the library login; give Claude your notes and the metadata.

## What a free SFPL card gets you

**Eligibility and process (as of 2026-10-15).** "Membership is free to all California residents." Apply online or on paper, then "present a valid photo ID in-person at any SFPL location" ([SFPL](https://sfpl.org/welcome-new-cardholders)). No proof of address is asked. Californians without California ID can get a **Welcome card** (online resources, one physical item at a time) or, if they cannot verify their name, a **Digital Access card** ("full access to computers and online resources", no physical borrowing, one year). Visitors from outside California can buy a **Visitor card** with photo ID and a temporary California address: "Visitor cards cost \$10 and are valid for 3 months". Neither FAQ says whether the Visitor card includes e-resources ([SFPL FAQ](https://sfpl.libanswers.com/faq/129952); [SFPL FAQ](https://sfpl.libanswers.com/faq/65116)).

SFPL's A–Z database list has 228 entries over 23 pages ([SFPL Articles & Databases](https://sfpl.org/articles-databases), October 2026). The ones that matter for this wiki's readers:

### O'Reilly through the library

- **The catalog is narrower than its reputation.** O'Reilly's public book sitemap lists 66,609 title URLs as of 2026-10-15 ([sitemap](https://www.oreilly.com/book-sitemap.xml)). By ISBN prefix, roughly 9,200 are Wiley; 9,000 O'Reilly (about 2,900 of them translations); 6,200 Pearson/Addison-Wesley; 4,000 Apress; 3,000 CRC/Taylor & Francis; 1,500 De Gruyter; and 1,100 Manning. Packt's ISBN range holds at most 6,443 (about 4,700 in identifiable Packt blocks). There are **zero MIT Press, Cambridge, Oxford or core Springer titles**, so no CLRS, Goodfellow, Sutton & Barto or PRML. (The prefix-to-publisher mapping is inferred, as corrected in R03's verification pass.)
- **Library edition versus full catalog.** SFPL's blurb says "50,000+" titles against 66,609 sitemap entries, of which about 1,300 are probably video or audio. Early-release books and live events may be excluded. I put it at [~70%] that nearly all *books* in the sitemap are readable through the library; nobody in this research logged in to check.
- **One O'Reilly card is enough.** Oakland, Berkeley ("O'Reilly Technical Books"), San José and Santa Clara County also list O'Reilly ([Oakland](https://oaklandlibrary.org/resource/), [Berkeley](https://www.berkeleypubliclibrary.org/explore/elibrary/alphabetical-list), [San José](https://www.sjpl.org/eresources/), [SCCLD](https://sccld.org/online-library/)). If the library editions are the same product, a second O'Reilly login adds nothing [~85%] except a backup if one library drops the license.
- **The terms probably bind you.** O'Reilly's membership agreement (updated 2024-10-09) bans "Web spiders" and AI training. It forbids storing or transmitting content "as input or a prompt to a machine learning or AI system" without "the express prior written permission of the copyright owner", and it caps quotes at 250 words ([O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/membership-agreement/), checked 2026-10-15). Users who reach the service through a Sponsor's agreement are bound by the same agreement plus any terms the Sponsor adds. The agreement does not mention libraries. I infer that library patrons count as sponsored users [~75%]. If so, pasting an O'Reilly chapter into Claude breaches the contract. See [The Legal Map](/building/legal-map/).

**Decision.** If you were going to pay \$499/year for O'Reilly and you live in California, get the SFPL card instead. Pay only if you need what the Individual plan includes and the library edition may lack (early-release books, live events, interactive labs and sandboxes; [O'Reilly pricing](https://www.oreilly.com/pricing/), October 2026) or have no eligible library. Cloud labs are a Teams feature, not part of the Individual plan.

### JSTOR and the article databases

- JSTOR's fee page for public libraries says: "Remote access for public libraries is not currently supported" and "JSTOR for public libraries is limited to walk-in access" ([JSTOR](https://about.jstor.org/products/fees/public-libraries/), checked 2026-10-15).
- SFPL's database list nonetheless links JSTOR through `ezproxy.sfpl.org`, and Berkeley PL lists JSTOR/Artstor behind its own EZproxy login ([SFPL](https://sfpl.org/articles-databases); [Berkeley PL](https://www.berkeleypubliclibrary.org/explore/elibrary/alphabetical-list)).
- LAPL marks JSTOR "Only available at LAPL Locations" ([LAPL](https://lapl.org/digital-library/search)).

Possible explanations: a legacy agreement, a proxy restricted to in-library IP addresses, or a stale listing. I put it at [~50%] that SFPL's link works from home. Even a working link may reach only part of JSTOR: public libraries can license the full archive or individual packages such as Collection I (arts and sciences) and Collection III (primary sources) ([JSTOR](https://about.jstor.org/products/fees/public-libraries/)). Test it with your own card before buying JPASS. If it fails, JSTOR's free account still gives 10 articles every 30 days, read online; see [Open Papers and Preprints](/access/open-papers/).

**Physics journals at Oakland and Berkeley.** Both list American Physical Society journals. Berkeley PL labels *Physical Review* "in library access only". Oakland links straight to journals.aps.org with no proxy login, so access is probably walk-in only as well ([Berkeley PL](https://www.berkeleypubliclibrary.org/explore/elibrary/alphabetical-list); [Oakland](https://oaklandlibrary.org/resource/), October 2026). That makes them a free walk-in route to APS journals; whether it works on your own laptop over library Wi-Fi was not checked.

**Expectations for papers.** Public-library article databases are general-purpose aggregators. For ML, statistics and forecasting papers, the legal open-access cascade (arXiv, Unpaywall, author copies) will usually beat them [~70%, inference]. They earn their place for social-science and humanities journals and for newspapers and trade press. University routes cover the rest: see [University and Employer Access](/access/academic-and-employer/).

### The Libby card-stack effect

Libby searches all your cards together, and each library buys its own ebook copies. The R03 spot-check queried OverDrive's catalog for each title at each library on 2026-10-15 and counted ebook editions with at least one owned copy ([OverDrive Thunder API example](https://thunder.api.overdrive.com/v2/libraries/sfpl/media?query=designing%20data-intensive%20applications)). The humanities column was broken out by library in a re-run the same day, with the same criterion; its totals (10, 16, 19) match R03's.

| Cards in Libby (cumulative) | Technical, Libby only (n = 33) | Technical, with SFPL O'Reilly + free copies | Humanities and social science (n = 20) |
|---|---|---|---|
| SFPL alone | 10 | 28 | 10 |
| + San José | 16 | 28 | 10 |
| + Santa Clara County | 17 | 28 | 14 |
| + Peninsula | 18 | 28 | 15 |
| + Berkeley | 18 | 28 | 16 |
| **+ Oakland (all six Bay Area systems)** | **18** | **28** | **16** |
| **+ LAPL** | **23** | **29** | **19** |
| + NYPL, Brooklyn | 23 (no gain) | 29 | not checked |

Increments are order-dependent; each row credits shared titles to the libraries above it. Technical: San José added *Hands-On ML*, *Designing ML Systems*, *AI Engineering*, *The Rust Programming Language*, *The Pragmatic Programmer* and *SICP*; Santa Clara County added *How to Measure Anything* (SFPL has only the audiobook); Peninsula added Raschka's *Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)*. Humanities: Santa Clara County added Kuhn's *Structure*, Anderson's *Imagined Communities*, Simler and Hanson's *The Elephant in the Brain* and Mokyr's *A Culture of Growth*; Peninsula added Polanyi's *The Great Transformation*; Berkeley added Rawls. Oakland added nothing in either sample (it holds several of these titles only as audiobooks). Hacking's *The Emergence of Probability* was missing everywhere. Matching was automatic (title plus author), so a few false negatives are possible.

**What the stack is worth.** All eight technical titles the Bay Area cards added were already on O'Reilly or free online, which is why the middle column never moves. For a technical reader the extra cards buy two things: Libby editions, which reach Kindle and Kobo e-readers where O'Reilly cannot (below), and a fallback if SFPL drops its O'Reilly license. For a humanities reader the stack is a real gain, 10 to 16 of 20, and most of it comes from Santa Clara County.

**What LAPL adds.** LAPL holds MIT Press ebooks that no Bay Area system had: CLRS (3e and 4e, 3 copies each), Goodfellow's *Deep Learning* (3) and Sutton & Barto (1), plus Pearson's *Clean Code* and Manning's *Deep Learning with Python*. The R03 verification pass re-confirmed the MIT Press holdings. But Goodfellow and Sutton & Barto are free online, and *Clean Code* and Chollet are on O'Reilly, so LAPL's net technical gain is one title, CLRS (28 to 29 of 33). A used CLRS 3e cost \$16.25 shipped on 2026-10-15 ([AbeBooks](https://www.abebooks.com/servlet/SearchResults?isbn=9780262033848&sortby=17)). LAPL's larger gains are humanities (+3 of 20 over the Bay stack: Parfit's *Reasons and Persons*, Weber's *Protestant Ethic*, Popper's *Logic of Scientific Discovery*) and its research databases (below). **Decision:** get an LAPL card on your next trip to Los Angeles. It is not worth a dedicated trip for technical reading.

**Holds are the real price.** A snapshot on 2026-10-15:

| Title | Library | Copies | Holds |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Thinking, Fast and Slow* | SFPL | 13 | 51 |
| *Thinking, Fast and Slow* | LAPL | 16 | 87 |
| *Guns, Germs, and Steel* | LAPL | 7 | 35 |
| *Thinking in Systems* | SFPL / LAPL | 26 / 18 | 14 / 30 |
| *AI Engineering* | LAPL / San José | 6 / 5 | 16 / 9 |
| *Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)* | LAPL | 3 | 20 |
| *The Alignment Problem* | SFPL | 7 | 8 |
| *Human Compatible* | SFPL | 2 | 9 |

A rough estimate of the wait for a new hold is (holds ÷ copies) × loan length. For *Thinking, Fast and Slow* at SFPL that is 51 ÷ 13 ≈ 3.9 loan periods: about 8 weeks at 14-day loans or 12 at 21-day loans, if every borrower keeps the book for the full term. Early returns and skipped holds shorten the real wait; pickup windows lengthen it [inference; I did not check SFPL's loan lengths]. With holds at several libraries, your wait is roughly the *minimum* of the queues [inference], which is where the card stack pays off for popular trade books. The stack does nothing for a title no library licenses, like *Gödel, Escher, Bach*, which may have no ebook edition.

**Unusual licenses.** Santa Clara County showed **334 copies** of the *AI Engineering* audiobook, 333 of them available, and San José showed 50 copies of a *Pragmatic Programmer* ebook edition, 49 available ([Thunder API](https://thunder.api.overdrive.com/v2/libraries/sanjose/media?query=the%20pragmatic%20programmer), 2026-10-15). Counts like these suggest cost-per-circulation or simultaneous-use licenses [~70%], so some technical titles may never have queues at some libraries.

**Reading Libby loans on e-ink.** Kindles receive Libby loans only through Libby's "Send to Kindle (U.S. only)" ([Libby on Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.overdrive.mobile.android.libby)). Kobo readers have OverDrive built in. Of the mainstream e-ink lines, only Android tablets (Boox) run both Libby and the O'Reilly app; Kindle and Kobo cannot run O'Reilly. Details are in [Reading Modes and Devices](/environments/reading-modes-and-devices/).

### LAPL's research databases

An LAPL card is free to California residents, collected in person in Los Angeles after optional online pre-registration ([LAPL](https://lapl.org/library-card)). LAPL lists 200+ databases and 1.5M+ ebooks and audiobooks. For researchers the notable ones are **ProQuest Central**, ProQuest Research Library, ABI/Inform, **OECD iLibrary**, **HeinOnline**, Lexis Advance, Westlaw, Oxford Reference, Gale eBooks and LinkedIn Learning. JSTOR is in-library only, and **there is no O'Reilly** ([LAPL research databases](https://lapl.org/digital-library/research); [LAPL resource list](https://lapl.org/digital-library/search), October 2026). I did not check which databases work remotely.

LAPL's pages say "Licensing agreements with our digital content vendors require that only Los Angeles City residents can receive an e-card" ([LAPL borrowing policies](https://lapl.org/library-card/borrowing-policies-and-information), checked 2026-10-15). They do not say whether a full card issued in person to a Californian living outside the City includes Libby and remote databases. Since the restriction is worded about e-cards, I put it at [~80%] that it does. Ask at the desk when you collect the card.

## Out-of-area access is shrinking

The standard advice used to be: buy a fee card from a big library in another state, or get LAPL's instant e-card, and read remotely. Both routes are narrowing.

| Library | Route | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LAPL** | Instant e-card | **Los Angeles City residents only**, per vendor licensing. I could not date the change | [LAPL](https://lapl.org/library-card/borrowing-policies-and-information) |
| LAPL | Full card | Free to California residents; **\$50 for applicants outside California**, issued and renewed in person only | same |
| **Orange County Library System (Orlando, FL)** | National fee card, which had included remote databases and ebook holds | "No longer being issued or renewed, as of July 31, 2026" | [OCLS](https://ocls.org/using-library/types-cards/what-are-types-cards/) |
| San Antonio PL | Bexar County residents outside city limits | New **\$30/year** fee from 2026-10-01, paid in person | [SAPL](https://guides.mysapl.org/librarycards) |
| **Santa Clara County Library District** | Any US mailing address | **Free full card, in person** (counter-example) | [SCCLD](https://sccld.org/card/) |
| SFPL | Visitor card for visitors from outside California (photo ID plus a temporary California address) | \$10 for 3 months; e-resource access not stated | [SFPL FAQ](https://sfpl.libanswers.com/faq/129952) |
| Brooklyn PL, Free Library of Philadelphia, Fairfax County | Fee cards | **Could not verify**: the sites returned 403/404 to automated requests on 2026-10-15. From pre-2026 knowledge, not re-checked, Brooklyn and Philadelphia sold non-resident cards for about \$50/year [unverified, low confidence] | — |

All statuses are as of 2026-10-15.

**The pattern.** E-content vendors increasingly tie licenses to the population a library serves. LAPL says so explicitly, and JSTOR's walk-in-only public-library model applies the same logic to databases. Libraries are closing the remote-only, out-of-area routes first. I put it at [~70%] that at least one more widely recommended non-resident or e-card route in the US closes or narrows by the end of 2027. The evidence is thin: two closures (LAPL's e-card restriction and OCLS) and one new fee (San Antonio) in a single snapshot, no trend data, and several pages that could not be checked.

**What to do about it.**

- Get in-person cards while they are offered, and renew before they lapse. Several renewals need a visit: Berkeley (expired cards), LAPL's non-resident card, and Oakland's unverified online card, which cannot be renewed online.
- Do not build a reading workflow on a single remote-only fee card.
- If you live outside California and visit the Bay Area, get the SCCLD card. If you visit Los Angeles from outside California, LAPL's \$50 card is the paid option.
- Keep your own list of routes with last-verified dates and re-check it yearly; this table will go stale.

## Link+ and interlibrary loan

Libby covers ebooks. For print-only books (no ebook edition, or no library license), California's public libraries have two lending networks.

| Route | Reach | Limits and costs (Oct 2026) | Speed | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Link+** | "over 11 million items from 64 libraries throughout California and Nevada" (Oakland's description). The member list shows 64 libraries, all in California (three marked "coming soon"): 59 public and 5 academic (Santa Clara University, Loyola Marymount, Pacific Union College, Napa Valley College, Solano Community College) | Oakland: 21-day loans with one 21-day renewal; no late fees; **\$115** for a lost or significantly damaged item. Berkeley PL: up to **25** Link+ items at once, 21 days, renewable once. SFPL participates (one renewal, 3 weeks) | "Most LINK+ requests arrive in less than one week" | [Oakland](https://oaklandlibrary.org/borrow-from-another-library/); [Link+ members](https://csul.iii.com/screens/members.html); [Berkeley PL](https://www.berkeleypubliclibrary.org/explore/linkplus); [SFPL FAQ](https://sfpl.libanswers.com/faq/85266) |

**Order of operations for a print-only book.**

1. Search your home library's catalog, then request through Link+. It is free and usually takes under a week; Berkeley PL lets you borrow up to 25 Link+ items at once.
2. If Link+ has nothing, use interlibrary loan. It is rationed (3 a month at Oakland) and sometimes charged, so save it for things Link+ cannot supply, journal articles included.
3. If you will annotate the book or need it for more than six weeks, buy used. On 2026-10-15 the cheapest shipped AbeBooks copies of the spot-check's residual titles were *Gödel, Escher, Bach* \$8.23, AIMA 4e \$33.15 (new, probably an international edition), CS:APP 3e \$31.54 (new) and Jaynes \$69.99 ([AbeBooks](https://www.abebooks.com/servlet/SearchResults?isbn=9780521592710&sortby=17)).

I did not check which Link+ libraries hold those residual titles. Because Link+ is almost all public libraries, expect it to be weaker for graduate texts like Jaynes or CS:APP than for trade books [~70%, inference]; that is where an interlibrary-loan request or a university walk-in visit earns its place.

## Get the cards that matter in one afternoon

The six Bay Area systems split into two loops: the Peninsula and South Bay (Peninsula Library System, San José, Santa Clara County) and the East Bay (Oakland, Berkeley). The Peninsula–South Bay loop supplied all eight extra technical Libby titles and five of the six extra humanities titles. After that loop, the East Bay added one more (Rawls, at Berkeley), plus Berkeley's JSTOR/Artstor link and both libraries' in-library physics journals.

- **Technical reader, reading on a laptop or Boox:** SFPL alone gives the measured coverage. The loop is optional.
- **Technical reader on Kindle or Kobo:** add San José, for Libby editions of six titles otherwise available only through O'Reilly or the free web.
- **Humanities reader, or a reader from outside California:** add Santa Clara County first (+4 humanities titles; the only card confirmed open to any US address).
- **Time for the full loop:** do SFPL plus the Peninsula–South Bay loop, and the East Bay another day.

**Costs.** For a California resident every card is **\$0** plus transit. For a non-Californian, only SCCLD is confirmed free; PLS states no residency rule; the others require California residence (as of 2026-10-15).

**The night before (about 30 minutes).**

1. **SFPL:** submit the online application ([SFPL](https://sfpl.org/welcome-new-cardholders)).
2. **Oakland:** apply online ([OPL](https://oaklandlibrary.org/get-a-library-card/)). You can use most of its e-resources at once. The card expires in 30 days unless you verify it; outside Oakland, Emeryville and Piedmont that means a branch visit.
3. **San José:** submit the online application ([SJPL](https://www.sjpl.org/membership/)).
4. **Peninsula Library System:** register online and pick the member library on your route ([PLS](https://plsinfo.org/get-a-library-card/)). The new account expires in 90 days if you do not finish in person.
5. **LAPL:** pre-register for your next trip to Los Angeles ([LAPL](https://lapl.org/library-card)).
6. **Documents:** photo ID plus proof of current address (a California address for San José). SFPL needs only the photo ID.

**The afternoon, Peninsula–South Bay loop (by car is simplest).** Increments are cumulative in this order.

| Stop | What to bring | You leave with | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Any SFPL branch | Photo ID | O'Reilly, Libby, LinkedIn Learning, Udemy, ProQuest eBook Central, EBSCO and Gale databases, JSTOR link, newspapers, Link+ | \$0 |
| 2. A Peninsula Library System member library | Photo ID + proof of address | Libby card #2: +2 technical, +1 humanities | \$0 |
| 3. Any San José PL branch | Photo ID + proof of California address | Libby card #3: +5 technical, 0 humanities; backup O'Reilly | \$0 |
| 4. Any SCCLD branch (for example Milpitas or Cupertino) | Photo ID with current address | Libby card #4: +1 technical, +4 humanities; The Great Courses; backup O'Reilly | \$0 |

The order can run in either direction. Check each branch's hours before you go.

**The East Bay loop (another afternoon, by BART).** Oakland's Main Library, to verify the online card if you live outside Oakland, Emeryville and Piedmont, and Berkeley's Central Library, where "applicants must be present". Both are near BART stations. Go for the in-library physics journals, Berkeley's JSTOR/Artstor link and Rawls; beyond the first loop, neither library added other titles in the samples. **Total for six Bay Area cards: \$0 plus fares.** Add LAPL (\$0 for Californians) on any trip to Los Angeles.

**The evening after (about 45 minutes).**

1. **Add every card to Libby.** Berkeley PL requires a PIN for Libby from 2026-10-15, so set one when you get that card. Turn on hold notifications.
2. **Activate O'Reilly once,** from the link on SFPL's database list.
3. **Set the SFPL PIN** if you want Statista (required from 2026-04-01).
4. **Test JSTOR from home** through SFPL's link and record whether it works. That settles a [~50%] question for your own planning.
5. **Place holds at every library** that has a book you want, and take the first copy that arrives.
6. **Put the expiry dates in your calendar:** Oakland 3 years; Berkeley and San José 4 years; Oakland online card 30 days if unverified; PLS online registration 90 days.

**Expected payoff.** If your reading resembles the spot-check samples: about 85% of a technical list (with legal free copies; SFPL alone gets you there) and about 80% of a humanities list as ebooks (this needs the stack), at \$0, with weeks of holds on popular trade titles. The remainder goes to Link+, interlibrary loan, [cheap subscriptions](/access/subscriptions/) such as Perlego for academic monographs (Bay Area Libby plus Perlego reached 18/20 humanities titles), or a used copy. The [Access Ladder](/access/access-ladder/) puts these routes in order.

## For tool builders

Public libraries are the largest free *content* source for a personal catalog that routes each work to wherever it can be read. They are also the most tightly licensed.

- **Availability data is reachable.** The Libby back end (OverDrive's undocumented "Thunder" catalog API) returns owned copies, available copies and holds count per title per library. Library keys found by R03: `sfpl`, `oakland`, `berkeleypubliclibrary`, `sanjose`, `santaclara`, `pls`, `lapl`, `nypl`, `brooklyn`. `berkeley` is Berkeley Law's library, not Berkeley PL. Ebook records can show zero owned copies and a holds queue (Oakland's *Guns, Germs, and Steel* ebook did on 2026-10-15), so filter on owned copies, not on the record's presence. The API is undocumented and may change; query it at polite rates. R09 rates scraping public-library catalogs **yellow**: facts are free, but terms of service still bind, so use APIs, rate-limit and honor robots.txt ([The Legal Map](/building/legal-map/)).
- **Two small modeling problems.** Choosing cards (and month-long subscription bursts) for a reading list is weighted set cover; the card-stack table above is a hand-run instance. Forecasting holds is a queueing problem: poll copies and holds daily, publish "P(available within 7 days)" per title per library, and score it with Brier scores against actual pickup dates.
- **Route to the content; do not ingest it.** O'Reilly forbids AI input. JSTOR's terms (2026-04-24) ban "systematically" downloading and using content "to train large language models" ([JSTOR terms](https://about.jstor.org/terms/)). A catalog can show "Libby SJPL, 0 holds" or "O'Reilly via SFPL" and deep-link there. The licensed text stays behind the library login. See [A Reference Architecture](/building/reference-architecture/) for the license-firewall layer.

## Open questions

- **Does SFPL's JSTOR EZproxy link work from home?** A cardholder can test it in five minutes. If it does, is it full JSTOR or one of the packages JSTOR sells public libraries (Collection I, II or III)?
- **Is O'Reilly for Public Libraries the full book catalog?** Sample 200 ISBNs from the sitemap and check each through a library login. The current estimate is [~70%] that nearly all books are included.
- **Do LAPL's in-person cards give non-City Californians Libby and remote databases?** Ask LAPL or test with a card. The estimate is [~80%] yes.
- **Does the O'Reilly membership agreement bind library patrons verbatim,** or do library-access terms differ? Find the library-edition terms page or ask a library's e-resources librarian.
- **What do SFPL's \$10 Visitor card and SJPL's Limited Status card include?** Specifically, do they reach Libby and O'Reilly? Both could matter to visitors from out of state.
- **How well do the hand-picked samples predict coverage?** Replace both sets with a random sample of a few hundred titles drawn from syllabi, and re-run the per-library breakdown, to measure coverage without fame bias.
- **How well does the holds-queue arithmetic predict real waits?** Log hold dates and pickup dates across a few dozen holds and compare them with Libby's own estimates and with (holds ÷ copies) × loan length.
- **Which Link+ libraries hold the residual print-only titles** (Jaynes, CS:APP, AIMA, *Gödel, Escher, Bach*)? A Link+ catalog search answers it, and tests whether a mostly public network covers graduate texts.
- **Do Brooklyn PL and the Free Library of Philadelphia still sell non-resident cards with e-access,** and at what price? Both blocked automated checks; a manual visit to their pages resolves it.
