Many library e-resource licenses are scoped to the population a library serves (LAPL says so explicitly, below). California's big urban systems define that population generously: most give a free card to any California resident. So a researcher in the Bay Area can collect several overlapping library licenses for the price of a few desk visits. This chapter covers what those cards actually unlock, how much each additional card adds, where the free routes are closing, and how to get the cards that matter in one afternoon.

## The short version

- **Get a San Francisco Public Library (SFPL) card first.** It is free to every California resident. You apply online and then show photo ID at any branch ([SFPL](https://sfpl.org/welcome-new-cardholders), as of October 2026). Its O'Reilly login alone stands in for a \$499/year individual O'Reilly plan ([O'Reilly pricing](https://www.oreilly.com/pricing/), October 2026), assuming the library edition carries nearly all the books [~70%]. In a 33-book technical spot-check, SFPL's O'Reilly and Libby together with legal free copies covered **28/33 (85%)**.
- **For technical ebooks, the O'Reilly login is worth more than Libby.** O'Reilly held 14 of the 33 titles. SFPL's Libby held 10, mostly trade books (*Superforecasting*, *The Book of Why*).
- **Stack cards for Libby.** Across six Bay Area systems, Libby held ebooks of **18/33 technical and 16/20 humanities titles**, against 10/33 and 10/20 for SFPL alone. In the technical sample, San José was the most valuable second card (+6 titles). Oakland and Berkeley added no technical titles beyond SFPL, but they spread holds on popular books and carry JSTOR (Berkeley) and walk-in physics journals.
- **If you are not a California resident,** Santa Clara County Library District (SCCLD) gives a **free full-access card to anyone with a US mailing address** who applies in person ([SCCLD](https://sccld.org/card/), October 2026). It is the one free Bay Area route open to out-of-state readers that I could verify.
- **Out-of-area remote access is shrinking.** LAPL's instant e-card is now for Los Angeles City residents only, citing vendor licensing ([LAPL](https://lapl.org/library-card/borrowing-policies-and-information)). Orlando's Orange County Library System stopped issuing fee cards on 2026-07-31 ([OCLS](https://ocls.org/using-library/types-cards/what-are-types-cards/)). Plan on cards that require a visit, and get them while they are offered.
- **JSTOR through a public library is officially walk-in only** ([JSTOR](https://about.jstor.org/products/fees/public-libraries/)). Yet SFPL and Berkeley list remote proxy links. I put it at [~50%] that an SFPL card gets you JSTOR from home today.
- **For print-only books, use Link+ before interlibrary loan, and both before buying.** Link+ reaches 64 libraries and 11M+ items; most requests arrive in under a week, with no late fees ([Oakland PL](https://oaklandlibrary.org/borrow-from-another-library/)).
- **Library access does not license AI use.** O'Reilly's membership agreement forbids using its content "as input or a prompt to a machine learning or AI system" ([O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/membership-agreement/), updated 2024-10-09). Read with the library login; keep Claude on your notes and the metadata.

People without California ID can buy a visitor card: "Visitor cards cost \$10 and are valid for 3 months" ([SFPL FAQ](https://sfpl.libanswers.com/faq/65116)). The FAQ does not say whether the visitor card includes e-resources.

### O'Reilly through the library

- **The catalog is narrower than its reputation.** O'Reilly's public book sitemap lists 66,609 title URLs as of 2026-10-15 ([sitemap](https://www.oreilly.com/book-sitemap.xml)). By ISBN prefix, roughly 9,200 are Wiley, 9,000 O'Reilly, 6,200 Pearson/Addison-Wesley, at most 6,400 in the Packt range, 4,000 Apress, 3,000 CRC/Taylor & Francis, 1,500 De Gruyter and 1,100 Manning. It has **zero MIT Press, Cambridge, Oxford or core Springer titles**, so no CLRS, Goodfellow, Sutton & Barto or PRML. The prefix-to-publisher mapping is the dossier author's inference, corrected by the R03 verification pass.
- **Library edition versus full catalog.** SFPL's blurb says "50,000+" titles against 66,609 sitemap entries. Early-release books and live events may be excluded. I put it at [~70%] that nearly all *books* in the sitemap are readable through the library; nobody in this research logged in to check.
- **One O'Reilly card is enough.** Oakland, Berkeley ("O'Reilly Technical Books"), San José and Santa Clara County also list O'Reilly ([Oakland](https://oaklandlibrary.org/resource/), [Berkeley](https://www.berkeleypubliclibrary.org/explore/elibrary/alphabetical-list), [San José](https://www.sjpl.org/eresources/), [SCCLD](https://sccld.org/online-library/)). If the library editions are the same product, a second O'Reilly login adds nothing [~85%]. Its value is as a backup if one library drops the license.
- **The terms probably bind you.** O'Reilly's membership agreement (updated 2024-10-09) bans "Web spiders" and AI training. It forbids storing or transmitting content "as input or a prompt to a machine learning or AI system" without the copyright holder's written permission, and it caps quotes at 250 words ([O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/membership-agreement/)). Sponsored users are governed by the same agreement plus the sponsor's terms. I infer that library patrons count as sponsored users [~75%]. If so, pasting an O'Reilly chapter into Claude is a breach of contract. A reading tool should give Claude the table of contents, public metadata and your own notes, not the licensed text. See [The Legal Map](/building/legal-map/).

**Decision.** If you were going to pay \$499/year for O'Reilly and you live in California, get the SFPL card instead. Pay for O'Reilly only if you need features the library edition may lack (early releases, live events, cloud labs) or have no eligible library.

### JSTOR and the article databases

The JSTOR question is the one real contradiction in the public-library picture.

- JSTOR's fee page for public libraries says: "Remote access for public libraries is not currently supported" and "JSTOR for public libraries is limited to walk-in access" ([JSTOR](https://about.jstor.org/products/fees/public-libraries/), October 2026).
- SFPL's database list nonetheless links JSTOR through `ezproxy.sfpl.org`, and Berkeley PL lists JSTOR/Artstor behind its own EZproxy login ([SFPL](https://sfpl.org/articles-databases); [Berkeley PL](https://www.berkeleypubliclibrary.org/explore/elibrary/alphabetical-list)).
- LAPL marks JSTOR "Only available at LAPL Locations" ([LAPL](https://lapl.org/digital-library/search)).

Possible explanations: a legacy agreement, a proxy restricted to in-library IP addresses, or a stale listing. I lean toward [~50%] that SFPL's link works from home. Test it with your own card before buying JPASS. If it fails, JSTOR's free account still gives 10 articles every 30 days, read online; see [Open Papers and Preprints](/access/open-papers/).

**Physics journals at Oakland and Berkeley.** Both list American Physical Society journals. Berkeley PL labels *Physical Review* "in library access only". Oakland links straight to journals.aps.org with no proxy login, so access is probably walk-in only as well ([Berkeley PL](https://www.berkeleypubliclibrary.org/explore/elibrary/alphabetical-list); [Oakland](https://oaklandlibrary.org/resource/), October 2026). This is a free route to the APS backfile for anyone willing to bring a laptop to the library.

**Expectations for papers.** Public-library article databases are general-purpose aggregators. For ML, statistics and forecasting papers, the legal open-access cascade (arXiv, Unpaywall, author copies) will usually beat them [~70%, inference]. They earn their place for social-science and humanities journals and for newspapers and trade press. University routes cover the rest: see [University and Employer Access](/access/academic-and-employer/).

### The Libby card-stack effect

Libby lets you add several library cards and search them together. Each library buys its own ebook copies, so coverage grows with every card. The R03 spot-check queried OverDrive's catalog for each title at each library on 2026-10-15 and counted ebook editions with at least one owned copy ([OverDrive Thunder API example](https://thunder.api.overdrive.com/v2/libraries/sfpl/media?query=designing%20data-intensive%20applications)).

| Cards in Libby | Technical (n = 33) | Humanities and social science (n = 20) |
|---|---|---|
| SFPL alone | 10 | 10 |
| SFPL + San José | 16 | not computed |
| SFPL + San José + Santa Clara County + Peninsula | 18 | not computed |
| **All six Bay Area systems** (adding Oakland and Berkeley) | **18** | **16** |
| **+ LAPL** | **23** | **19** |
| + NYPL, Brooklyn | 23 (no gain) | — |

The per-library increments in the technical column are my computation from the dossier's title-by-library table. San José alone added six titles over SFPL: *Hands-On ML*, *Designing ML Systems*, *AI Engineering*, *The Rust Programming Language*, *The Pragmatic Programmer* and *SICP*. Santa Clara County added *How to Measure Anything* (SFPL has only the audiobook). Peninsula added Raschka's *Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)*. In this sample, Oakland and Berkeley held only titles SFPL already had. The humanities increments by library were not broken out; Hacking's *The Emergence of Probability* was missing everywhere.

**What LAPL adds.** LAPL holds MIT Press ebooks that no Bay Area system had: CLRS (3e and 4e, 3 copies each), Goodfellow's *Deep Learning* (3) and Sutton & Barto (1), plus Pearson's *Clean Code* and Manning's *Deep Learning with Python*. The R03 verification pass re-confirmed the MIT Press holdings. But Goodfellow and Sutton & Barto are already free online, so for the technical set LAPL's net gain over SFPL and the free web is one title, CLRS (28 to 29 of 33). A used CLRS 3e cost \$16.25 shipped on 2026-10-15 ([AbeBooks](https://www.abebooks.com/servlet/SearchResults?isbn=9780262033848&sortby=17)). LAPL's larger gain is humanities (+3 of 20) and its research databases (below). **Decision:** get an LAPL card on your next trip to Los Angeles. For technical reading alone it is not worth a dedicated trip.

**Holds are the real price.** A snapshot on 2026-10-15 (copies / holds):

| Title | Library | Copies | Holds |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Thinking, Fast and Slow* | SFPL | 13 | 51 |
| *Thinking, Fast and Slow* | LAPL | 16 | 87 |
| *Guns, Germs, and Steel* | LAPL | 7 | 35 |
| *Thinking in Systems* | SFPL / LAPL | 26 / 18 | 14 / 30 |
| *AI Engineering* | LAPL / San José | 6 / 5 | 16 / 9 |
| *Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)* | LAPL | 3 | 20 |
| *The Alignment Problem* | SFPL | 7 | 8 |
| *Human Compatible* | SFPL | 2 | 9 |

A crude upper bound on the wait for a new hold is (holds ÷ copies) × loan length. For *Thinking, Fast and Slow* at SFPL that is 51 ÷ 13 ≈ 3.9 loan periods: about 8 weeks at 14-day loans or 12 at 21-day loans, if every borrower keeps the book for the full term. Early returns and holds that get skipped make the real wait shorter [inference; I did not check SFPL's loan lengths]. With holds at several libraries, your wait is roughly the *minimum* of the queues [inference], which is where the card stack pays off for popular trade books. The stack does nothing for a title no library licenses, like *Gödel, Escher, Bach*, which may simply have no ebook edition.

**Unusual licenses.** Santa Clara County showed **334 copies** of the *AI Engineering* audiobook, 333 of them available. That pattern suggests a cost-per-circulation or simultaneous-use license, and some technical titles may never have queues at some libraries [I, med].

**Reading Libby loans on e-ink.** Kindles receive Libby loans only through Libby's "Send to Kindle (U.S. only)" ([Libby on Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.overdrive.mobile.android.libby)). Kobo readers have OverDrive built in. Android e-ink tablets (Boox) run Libby and the O'Reilly app natively. Details are in [Reading Modes and Devices](/environments/reading-modes-and-devices/).

### LAPL's research databases

An LAPL card is free to California residents, collected in person in Los Angeles after optional online pre-registration ([LAPL](https://lapl.org/library-card)). LAPL lists 200+ databases and 1.5M+ ebooks and audiobooks. For researchers the notable ones are **ProQuest Central**, ProQuest Research Library, ABI/Inform, **OECD iLibrary**, **HeinOnline**, Lexis Advance, Westlaw, Oxford Reference, Gale eBooks and LinkedIn Learning. JSTOR is in-library only, and **there is no O'Reilly** ([LAPL research databases](https://lapl.org/digital-library/research); [LAPL resource list](https://lapl.org/digital-library/search), October 2026). I did not check which databases work remotely.

One uncertainty matters here. LAPL's pages say e-cards are limited to Los Angeles City residents because "Licensing agreements with our digital content vendors require" it ([LAPL borrowing policies](https://lapl.org/library-card/borrowing-policies-and-information)). They do not say whether a full card issued in person to a Californian living outside the City includes Libby and remote databases. Since the restriction is worded about e-cards, I put it at [~80%] that it does. Ask at the desk when you collect the card.

## Out-of-area access is shrinking

For years the standard advice was to buy a fee card from a big library in another state, or to get LAPL's instant e-card, and read remotely. Both routes are narrowing.

| Library | Route | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LAPL** | Instant e-card | **Los Angeles City residents only**, per vendor licensing. I could not date the change | [LAPL](https://lapl.org/library-card/borrowing-policies-and-information) |
| LAPL | Full card | Free to California residents; **\$50 for applicants outside California**, issued and renewed in person only | same |
| **Orange County Library System (Orlando, FL)** | National fee card, which had included remote databases and ebook holds | "No longer being issued or renewed, as of July 31, 2026" | [OCLS](https://ocls.org/using-library/types-cards/what-are-types-cards/) |
| San Antonio PL | Bexar County residents outside city limits | New **\$30/year** fee from 2026-10-01, paid in person | [SAPL](https://guides.mysapl.org/librarycards) |
| **Santa Clara County Library District** | Any US mailing address | **Free full card, in person** (counter-example) | [SCCLD](https://sccld.org/card/) |
| SFPL | Visitor card for people without California ID | \$10 for 3 months; e-resource access not stated | [SFPL FAQ](https://sfpl.libanswers.com/faq/65116) |
| Houston PL | Non-resident card | No non-resident fee on its fee schedule. That shows only that no fee is listed, not that a card exists | [Houston PL](https://houstonlibrary.org/policies-fees) |
| Brooklyn PL, Free Library of Philadelphia, Fairfax County | Fee cards | **Could not verify**: the sites returned 403/404 to automated requests on 2026-10-15. Older reports put Brooklyn and Philadelphia near \$50/year [unverified, low confidence] | — |

All statuses are as of 2026-10-15.

**The pattern.** E-content vendors increasingly tie licenses to the population a library serves. LAPL says so explicitly, and JSTOR's walk-in-only public-library model is the same logic applied to databases. Libraries are closing the remote-only, out-of-area routes first. I put it at [~70%] that at least one more widely recommended non-resident or e-card route in the US closes or narrows by the end of 2027. The evidence is thin: two closures (LAPL's e-card restriction and OCLS) and one new fee (San Antonio) in a single snapshot, no trend data, and several pages that could not be checked.

**What to do about it.**

- Get in-person cards while they are offered, and renew before they lapse. Several renewals need a visit: Berkeley for an expired card, LAPL's non-resident card, and Oakland's online card, which cannot be renewed online.
- Do not build a reading workflow on a single remote-only fee card.
- If you live outside California and visit the Bay Area, SCCLD is the card to get. If you visit Los Angeles from outside California, LAPL's \$50 card is the paid option.
- Keep your own list of routes with last-verified dates and re-check it yearly; this table will go stale.

## Link+ and interlibrary loan

Libby covers ebooks. For print-only books (no ebook edition, or no library license), California's public libraries have two lending networks.

| Route | Reach | Limits and costs (Oct 2026) | Speed | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Link+** | "Over 11 million items from 64 libraries throughout California and Nevada" | Oakland: 21-day loans with one 21-day renewal; no late fees; **\$115** for a lost or significantly damaged item. Berkeley PL: up to **25** Link+ items at once, 21 days, renewable once. SFPL participates (one renewal, 3 weeks) | "Most LINK+ requests arrive in less than one week" | [Oakland](https://oaklandlibrary.org/borrow-from-another-library/); [Berkeley PL](https://www.berkeleypubliclibrary.org/explore/linkplus); [SFPL FAQ](https://sfpl.libanswers.com/faq/85266) |
| **Interlibrary loan (Oakland PL)** | Books and journal articles from libraries outside Link+ | **3 requests per month.** Excludes current-year books, items Oakland owns, anything available through Link+, and DVDs, CDs and audiobooks. "Not all materials are available for free"; the library checks with you before accepting a charge. \$115 minimum for a lost item | Not stated | [Oakland](https://oaklandlibrary.org/borrow-from-another-library/) |
| UC Berkeley and Stanford paid community cards | — | **Exclude** interlibrary borrowing | — | See [University and Employer Access](/access/academic-and-employer/) |

**Order of operations for a print-only book.**

1. Search your home library's catalog, then request through Link+. It is free and usually takes under a week; Berkeley PL lets you borrow up to 25 Link+ items at once.
2. If Link+ has nothing, use interlibrary loan. It is rationed (3 a month at Oakland) and sometimes charged, so save it for things Link+ cannot supply, journal articles included.
3. If you will annotate the book or need it for more than six weeks, buy used. On 2026-10-15 the cheapest shipped AbeBooks copies of the spot-check's residual titles were *Gödel, Escher, Bach* \$8.23, AIMA 4e \$33.15 (new, probably an international edition), CS:APP 3e \$31.54 (new) and Jaynes \$69.99 ([AbeBooks](https://www.abebooks.com/servlet/SearchResults?isbn=9780521592710&sortby=17)).

I did not check which Link+ libraries hold those residual titles. Link+ includes academic libraries [~85%; the member list was not fetched], which is where texts like Jaynes or CS:APP are most likely to be found.

## Get every card that matters in one afternoon

The six Bay Area systems split into two loops: the East Bay (Oakland, Berkeley) and the Peninsula and South Bay (Peninsula Library System, San José, Santa Clara County). In the technical spot-check, the **South Bay loop accounted for all eight Libby titles beyond SFPL**, and the East Bay added none. If you have one afternoon and read mostly technical books, do SFPL plus the South Bay loop. Do the East Bay later, on foot from BART, for JSTOR via Berkeley, the APS journals and more humanities holds.

**Costs.** For a California resident every card below is **\$0**; the cost is transit or driving. For a non-Californian, only SCCLD (free, any US mailing address) is confirmed; PLS states no residency rule; the rest require California residence. LAPL charges \$50 for out-of-state applicants. All as of 2026-10-15.

**The night before (about 30 minutes).**

1. **SFPL:** submit the online application ([SFPL](https://sfpl.org/welcome-new-cardholders)).
2. **Oakland:** apply online ([OPL](https://oaklandlibrary.org/get-a-library-card/)). You can use most of its e-resources at once. The card expires in 30 days unless you verify it; outside Oakland, Emeryville and Piedmont that means a branch visit.
3. **San José:** submit the online application ([SJPL](https://www.sjpl.org/membership/)).
4. **Peninsula Library System:** register online and pick the member library on your route ([PLS](https://plsinfo.org/get-a-library-card/)). The pending account lasts 90 days.
5. **LAPL:** pre-register for your next trip to Los Angeles ([LAPL](https://lapl.org/library-card)).
6. **Documents:** a photo ID, plus proof of current address (check each library's list of accepted documents). SCCLD asks for ID showing "your picture, name, and proof of your current address"; San José wants proof of a California address; PLS wants proof of current home address. SFPL asks only for photo ID.

**The afternoon, South Bay loop (by car is simplest).**

| Stop | What to bring | You leave with | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Any SFPL branch | Photo ID | O'Reilly, Libby, LinkedIn Learning, Udemy, ProQuest eBook Central, EBSCO and Gale databases, JSTOR link, newspapers, Link+ | \$0 |
| 2. A Peninsula Library System member library | Photo ID + proof of address | Libby card #2 (Raschka, *AI Engineering* in the sample) | \$0 |
| 3. Any San José PL branch | Photo ID + proof of California address | Libby card #3 (+6 technical titles in the sample), LinkedIn Learning, backup O'Reilly | \$0 |
| 4. Any SCCLD branch (for example Milpitas or Cupertino) | Photo ID with current address | Libby card #4, Great Courses, Academic Search Complete, backup O'Reilly; the one card open to any US address | \$0 |

The order can run in either direction. Check each branch's hours before you go. If you read mostly humanities, the per-library gains were not measured, so do whichever loop is closer first.

**The East Bay loop (another afternoon, by BART).** Oakland's Main Library to verify the online card, and Berkeley's Central Library, where "applicants must be present". Both are near BART stations. **Total for six Bay Area cards: \$0 plus fares.** Add LAPL (\$0 for Californians) on any trip to Los Angeles.

**The evening after (about 45 minutes).**

1. **Add every card to Libby.** Berkeley PL requires a PIN for Libby from 2026-10-15, so set one when you get that card. Turn on hold notifications.
2. **Activate O'Reilly once,** from the link on SFPL's database list.
3. **Set the SFPL PIN** if you want Statista (required from 2026-04-01).
4. **Test JSTOR from home** through SFPL's link and record whether it works. That settles a [~50%] question for your own planning.
5. **Place holds at every library** that has a book you want, and take the first copy that arrives.
6. **Put the expiry dates in your calendar:** Oakland 3 years; Berkeley and San José 4 years; Oakland online card 30 days if unverified; PLS registration 90 days.

**Expected payoff.** If your reading resembles the spot-check samples: about 85% of a technical list (with legal free copies) and about 80% of a humanities list as ebooks, at \$0, with weeks of holds on popular trade titles. The remainder goes to Link+, interlibrary loan, [cheap subscriptions](/access/subscriptions/) such as Perlego for academic monographs (Bay Area Libby plus Perlego reached 18/20 humanities titles), or a used copy. The [Access Ladder](/access/access-ladder/) puts these routes in order.

## For tool builders

Public libraries are the largest free *content* source for a personal catalog that routes each work to wherever it can be read. They are also the most tightly licensed.

- **Availability data is reachable.** The Libby back end (OverDrive's undocumented "Thunder" catalog API) returns owned copies, available copies and holds count per title per library. Library keys found by R03: `sfpl`, `oakland`, `berkeleypubliclibrary`, `sanjose`, `santaclara`, `pls`, `lapl`, `nypl`, `brooklyn`. `berkeley` is Berkeley Law's library, not Berkeley PL. The API is undocumented and may change. Query it at polite rates. R09 rates scraping public-library catalogs **yellow**: facts are free, terms of service still bind, so use APIs and rate limits and honor robots.txt ([The Legal Map](/building/legal-map/)).
- **Two small modeling problems.** Choosing cards (and month-long subscription bursts) for a reading list is weighted set cover; the card-stack table above is a hand-run instance. Forecasting holds is a queueing problem: poll copies and holds daily, publish "P(available within 7 days)" per title per library, and score it with Brier scores against actual pickup dates.
- **Route to the content; do not ingest it.** O'Reilly forbids AI input. JSTOR's terms (2026-04-24) ban "systematically" downloading and using content "to train large language models" ([JSTOR terms](https://about.jstor.org/terms/)). A catalog can show "Libby SJPL, 0 holds" or "O'Reilly via SFPL" and deep-link there. The licensed text stays behind the library login. See [A Reference Architecture](/building/reference-architecture/) for the license-firewall layer.

## Open questions

- **Does SFPL's JSTOR EZproxy link work from home?** A cardholder can test it in five minutes. If it does, is it full JSTOR or a subset of collections (JSTOR sells public libraries packages such as Collection I and III)?
- **Is O'Reilly for Public Libraries the full book catalog?** Sample 200 ISBNs from the sitemap and check each through a library login. The current estimate is [~70%] that nearly all books are included.
- **Do LAPL's in-person cards give non-City Californians Libby and remote databases?** Ask LAPL or test with a card. The estimate is [~80%] yes.
- **Does the O'Reilly membership agreement bind library patrons verbatim,** or do library-access terms differ? Find the library-edition terms page.
- **What do SFPL's \$10 visitor card and SJPL's Limited Status card include?** Both could matter to visitors from out of state.
- **How much does each Bay Area card add for humanities?** Re-run the 20-title set per library. Better, replace both hand-picked sets with a random sample of a few hundred titles drawn from syllabi, to measure coverage without fame bias.
- **How well does the holds-queue arithmetic predict real waits?** Log hold dates and pickup dates across a few dozen holds and compare them with Libby's own estimates and with (holds ÷ copies) × loan length.
- **Which Link+ libraries hold the residual print-only titles** (Jaynes, CS:APP, AIMA, *Gödel, Escher, Bach*)? A Link+ catalog search answers it.
- **Do Brooklyn PL and the Free Library of Philadelphia still sell non-resident cards with e-access,** and at what price? Both blocked automated checks; a manual visit to their pages resolves it.
